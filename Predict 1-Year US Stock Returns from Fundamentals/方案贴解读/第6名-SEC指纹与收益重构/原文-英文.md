## Overview

I finished **6th place** in the Kaggle competition **Predict 1-Year US Stock Returns from Fundamentals**, with a final private leaderboard score of **4421.10130** after 217 submissions and extensive experimentation.
Competition leaderboard: https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-1-year-us-stock-returns-from-fundamentals/leaderboard

The competition asked participants to predict one-year forward returns for US-listed stocks using financial fundamentals derived from SEC filings. The training set contained 23,070 stock-quarter observations from 2019–2022, while the test set contained 8,520 anonymized observations from 2024.

This was an especially difficult problem because stock returns are noisy, non-stationary, and strongly influenced by a small number of extreme outcomes. Since the evaluation metric was mean squared error, a single incorrectly predicted multi-bagger could dominate the total score.

## The central challenge

The test companies were represented by anonymous identifiers such as:

```asciidoc
STOCK_0001
STOCK_0002
STOCK_0003
```

This removed the direct connection between each test row and its real company ticker.
However, accounting statements contain highly distinctive combinations of values. A company's revenue, assets, equity, debt, inventory, goodwill, shares outstanding, and profitability ratios can act as a financial fingerprint.

The key insight behind my solution was:

> Instead of treating every test row only as a generic feature vector, attempt to identify the underlying company and fiscal period using its SEC filing fingerprint.

## 2. Multi-pass company fingerprinting

A single matching rule was not sufficiently reliable, so I designed a multi-stage identity-resolution system.

### Pass 1: strict financial fingerprinting

Candidate companies were first filtered using the logarithm of total assets. They were then scored using exact and near-exact matches across multiple accounting fields.
The score emphasized:

- exact accounting-value matches;
- near-exact matches;
- agreement on derived ratios;
- uniqueness relative to the second-best company candidate.

The primary derived ratio checks included:

```abnf
ROE = net income / shareholders' equity
ROA = net income / total assets
Net margin = net income / revenue
```

A company was accepted only when the best candidate exceeded the alternatives by a sufficient margin.

### Pass 2: ratio-assisted matching

A second matching pass used a less restrictive accounting-field requirement but required stronger agreement on profitability ratios.
This increased coverage while preserving high precision.

### Pass 3: company-level chain matching

Some individual quarters were difficult to identify because of missing values or restatements.
Rather than matching each row independently, I compared the complete multi-quarter sequence belonging to an anonymous stock. A company was accepted when the full sequence of assets, equity, revenue, and other values aligned with a real ticker.

### Pass 6: vintage company-facts matching

SEC Frames generally expose the latest filed values. However, the competition data may have captured values before a later amendment or restatement.
To address this, the final matching stage searched historical company-fact values. Any value previously filed for a particular company and reporting period could contribute evidence.

## Identity-resolution validation

I validated the matching system by hiding known training tickers and attempting to recover them from the financial features.
The principal validation results were:

| Matching component | Validation result |
|---|---|
| Strict fingerprint ticker precision | 99.40% |
| Strict fingerprint period precision | 99.93% |
| Second-pass ticker precision | 99.55% |
| SEC Frames ticker precision | 97.57% |
| Frames-chain precision | 97.84% |
| Vintage matcher precision on evaluated rows | 100.00% |

These figures were calculated only on the rows for which each matching method produced an identity. They measure conditional precision rather than overall coverage.

## 3. Ownership and collision controls

Independent row matching can assign the same real ticker to multiple anonymous stocks.
To prevent this, I added several controls:

- company-level ownership verification;
- preference for strict fingerprint matches;
- rejection of mappings conflicting with stronger stock-level evidence;
- collision purging;
- propagation only when sibling quarters were financially consistent;
- separation of verified and unverifiable community mappings.

The pipeline initially mapped approximately 98% of test rows, but weaker and conflicting mappings were removed before return reconstruction.
The final accepted rows were divided into confidence tiers such as:

```excel
fp       — strict fingerprint
fp2      — second-pass fingerprint
chain    — company-sequence match
sec      — SEC Frames match
secv     — historical SEC filing-value match
map_hi   — high-confidence mapping
ann      — annual-statement match
uni      — unique compatible company
```

## 4. Fiscal-period reconstruction

Identifying the company was not enough. Each row also needed the correct start and end date for its one-year holding period.
I constructed fiscal calendars using:

- known training-period dates;
- EDGAR reporting periods;
- SEC filing dates;
- per-quarter external return files;
- historical fiscal-year patterns.

The expected end date was generated approximately one year after the start date and then aligned to the nearest known fiscal date within an allowed window.
On the training data, the reconstructed end date was:

- exactly correct for 98.99% of rows;
- within three days for 99.04% of rows.

## 5. Market-price return engine

Historical market prices were retrieved using Yahoo Finance, with Stooq used as a fallback for unavailable symbols.
The price layer handled:

- ticker formatting differences;
- stock splits;
- dividends;
- missing trading days;
- delisted or renamed securities;
- API rate limits;
- caching for reproducibility.

For each observation, prices were taken from the most recent trading day on or before the required date, subject to a maximum allowed gap.
I tested several return definitions.

### Split-adjusted price return

Raw closing prices were adjusted for stock splits while retaining a price-only interpretation.

### Adjusted-close return

Adjusted close was used when the split-adjusted raw series was unavailable or inconsistent.

### Dividend adjustment

When adjusted close was used, estimated dividend contribution was removed so the reconstructed value better matched the competition's price-return target.

Using known tickers and dates from the training set, the composite return engine achieved:

| Coverage | 96.3% |

This result showed that the price-return calculation itself was highly accurate when the ticker and period were correct.

## 6. Base-anchored prediction strategy

A fully reconstructed submission was too risky because even a small number of incorrect identities could produce catastrophic squared errors.
Instead, I used a strong existing submission as an anchor.
A reconstructed return replaced the base prediction only when:

- a valid price series was available;
- the ticker and period passed confidence checks;
- the return was within the permitted range for its confidence tier;
- the difference from the base prediction was not excessive for weaker tiers;
- the ticker was not included in a training-derived blacklist.

The highest-confidence fingerprint tiers were allowed greater freedom. SEC-based and mapping-based tiers received stricter limits.

## What did not work

### Pure fundamental regression

Tree ensembles and conventional tabular models were unable to approach the strongest leaderboard scores.
Fundamental variables contain some cross-sectional return information, but realized one-year returns remain extremely noisy. The target also contained very large outliers, making MSE particularly difficult.

### Aggressive identity coverage

Increasing mapping coverage without equally increasing precision usually reduced leaderboard performance.
For this task, identity precision was more valuable than raw coverage.

### Global prediction clipping

Clipping all extreme returns appeared attractive because the metric heavily penalized large errors.
However, some extreme returns were genuine. Removing a correctly reconstructed multi-bagger could be more damaging than retaining it.

### Independent quarter matching

Matching every row separately created identity collisions and inconsistent quarterly assignments. Company-level sequence matching was more stable.

## Public and private leaderboard behavior

The public and private leaderboard results differed significantly.
This was expected because a relatively small number of extreme-return observations could dominate the MSE on either split.
The private leaderboard exposed several weaknesses:

- remaining identity errors;
- incorrect quarter assignments;
- unresolved ticker changes;
- inherited extreme predictions;
- distribution differences between the public and private partitions.

Although the final private score was considerably worse than the best public experiments, the solution still finished **6th overall**.
This reinforced an important lesson:

> In a fat-tailed financial prediction problem, average validation accuracy is not enough. The most important rows are often the rare observations where an identity or period error produces a return difference of several hundred or several thousand percentage points.
