# 3rd Place: Fingerprinting, Return Reconstruction, and What Moved the Score

Source: https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/predict-1-year-us-stock-returns-from-fundamentals/writeups/3rd-place-fingerprinting-return-reconstruction
Author: Abdessamed Zetroni | Solution Writeup · 3rd place · Jul 20, 2026

Final: 3rd place, private LB 3,371.53. Public LB #2 at 102.45

Below is what actually moved the score, with the specific rules that worked, the one convention that quietly decides everything, and a post-competition test of the techniques I hadn't used.

---

## This was a lookup problem, not a modeling problem

The target was one-year realized price return for real, identifiable US companies in 2024. Tickers were masked, but a company's reported financials are a near-unique fingerprint, and the rules allowed external public data.

The number that tells you the whole story: an honest model on the 33 provided fundamentals tops out around **15,000 MSE**, barely ahead of predicting a constant. The leaderboard was three orders of magnitude below that. No model closes that gap. The only thing that does is resolving each masked company to its real ticker and reconstructing its actual 2024 return from public filings and prices. So that is the real problem, and everything below is about doing it precisely.

## Match on exact reported dollars, not ratios

Ratios (P/E, ROE, margins) collide constantly across companies and are rounded in the data. Raw reported dollar values (revenue, assets, equity, net income, shares outstanding) are effectively unique. Match on those.

The rules that held up:

- **Require at least two independent exact dollar-field agreements** before trusting a match. I tested trusting unique single-field matches to raise coverage. It regressed the score every time. One coincidental collision costs more than the row is worth.
- **Collisions are the real adversary.** Two unrelated companies can share one field (I had a wrong match born from a single identical `income_before_tax`). The fix that worked: accept a one-field bridge only if that company is the best match for at least two of its rows on at least two distinct values.
- **Match at the company level, not the row level.** A masked id is consistent across its quarters, so resolve the whole multi-quarter sequence together rather than each row alone. I assigned quarters with a Hungarian match. This alone rescued rows that were ambiguous in isolation.
- **Use vintage filings, not just the latest.** Amended and restated 10-Ks mean a live "latest value" source won't match the as-originally-filed competition value. SEC `companyfacts` (which retains historical filed values) recovered a batch of rows that the Frames API (latest-only) silently missed.

## The return convention is the trap that decides your score

Once you have the ticker and the holding window, you still have to compute the return the *host's way*. This is where most reconstructions leak error.

The target is a **price return, not a total return**. yfinance's Adjusted Close folds dividends back in, so using it systematically biases every dividend-payer upward. Using raw split-adjusted Close matched the host's definition: validated on the training labels at **correlation 0.998, median error ~0.00pp**. Getting this one choice right is worth more than any amount of modeling.

The window matters just as much: `period_start` is the matched fiscal quarter-end, `period_end` the same quarter one year later. A wrong window reconstructs a real return for the wrong dates.

## What an honest model actually buys you

For a lookup-free baseline I built a recency-weighted 2015–2025 fundamentals panel and reached a cross-sectional **rank-IC of about 0.145**. That is real, stable signal; fundamentals do carry some cross-sectional information.

It barely moved MSE (≈15,300 vs 15,765 for a constant). Because the metric is tail-dominated, IC improvements are nearly invisible on the leaderboard. Worth knowing before you spend a week tuning: on this metric, modeling skill and leaderboard rank are almost unrelated.

## What actually separated 1st from 3rd

Not a cleverer matching rule and not any single technique. My remaining gap to the top was almost entirely **coverage of the few hundred companies absent from every accessible source**, ones I could not resolve at all. On the rows I could resolve, reconstruction was excellent. The teams above me simply resolved more of the hard, source-absent companies. In a reconstruction competition the frontier is a broader identity source, not a smarter algorithm.
